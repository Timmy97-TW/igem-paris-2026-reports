Three advisers from GEMS Taiwan (Gabriel, wet lab; Chars, wet lab; Timmy, PI/dry lab) attend the
Paris Grand Jamboree and each owes a written research report afterwards. The reports are organised
around four themes:

- **C — Novel concepts & interdisciplinary.** How teams choose a real problem; niches that matter to
  Taiwan (agriculture, environment, food, sustainable materials); combinations of fields
  (data science, design, economics) that create a new kind of project.
- **H — Hardware & automation.** Low-cost instruments, open-source lab hardware, field-deployable
  kits; making tools portable, affordable, usable; 3D printing, microcontrollers, consumer parts.
- **M — Computation, modelling, data.** ML / simulation for structure, metabolic flux, ecosystem
  dynamics; data visualisation for public and judges; biological data turned into predictive tools.
- **S — Science for good.** Education and public-engagement formats, human practices that actually
  serve a community, plans to continue the work or bring it to users.

## Our own project, for judging relevance

**ReLeaf** — a closed-loop bioreactor running engineered *Bacillus subtilis*. Green/red light through
the CcaSR optogenetic system switches production of plant protectants (ACC deaminase; a peptide,
BoPep4), released through a membrane to plants under climate stress. Dry-lab side: in-line OD600 on
low-cost photodiodes, a digital twin, an anomaly-detection layer over the sensor stream.

Things we know we are weak on and want to learn from others: protein secretion in *B. subtilis*,
biocontainment and kill switches for released organisms, validation on real plants, and
measurement/calibration rigour.

## How to judge a team's entry

Relevance labels: `same space` (bioreactors, plant-protecting microbes, optogenetics, *B. subtilis*,
ACC deaminase, plant stress) · `technique to borrow` · `benchmark` · `new direction` ·
`none but instructive`.

Score 0–3 for how much a visit would teach these three. 0 = skip.

Abstracts are written by the teams to sell the project. Where a claim looks unsupported, say so —
that is usually the most useful question to ask at the poster.

## How we work on the floor

1. **One note per team, from the template.** Free-form notes written by three people do not merge.
2. **Mechanism, not claim.** "They built a turbidostat" is worth nothing. "They held OD by chopping
   the LED at 1 kHz and lock-in detecting, because greenhouse light swamped the DC signal" is a note.
3. **Mark where it came from.** What a team member told you, what you read off a poster, and what you
   worked out yourself are three different things. In six months it is the difference between a
   usable note and a rumour.
4. **Decide the relevance at the poster.** A note without a verdict is unfinished.
5. **Do not all walk the same aisle.** The [floor plan]({{ '/floor-plan.html' | relative_url }}) splits it three ways.
6. **Write it up the same evening.** Notes written on the flight home are a fraction of what you saw.
   Budget forty-five minutes a night.

## Photographs

Posters and booths are fine, and teams are usually pleased to be asked. **Ask before photographing
people, and ask before photographing anything that may be unpublished** — some teams are sitting on a
manuscript. "May I photograph this?" also opens the conversation that gets you the mechanism, which
is what you came for.

Three frames per team: the poster wide, the one figure that matters, and something with the team name
legible. Then the photos label themselves later.
